Create a short, exceptional animated trailer for **Yochi**, built entirely in code.

Yochi is a multiplayer prediction game where players wager USDC on whether the next market candle will go **UP or DOWN**. Players compete directly against one another across free-for-all markets, 1v1 Duels and Battle Royale games. Around that core are social systems: profiles, streaks, achievements, rivals, clans, XP, status and public wins.

I am providing you with a curated set of source assets, including:

- Yochi branding and logo assets
- the canonical colour palette / hex values
- screenshots of the actual product
- gameplay recordings showing FFA, Duels and Battle Royale
- profile, leaderboard, achievement and other social-system references
- character / avatar artwork where useful
- the final music track
- a waveform image
- a short timing map identifying the major musical beats, build, drop, high-impact moments and ending

Treat the supplied gameplay and UI primarily as **reference material for understanding Yochi**, not as footage that must appear literally in the finished film.

The goal is not to make a product walkthrough.

The goal is to make a **banger**.

This should be the kind of video that stops someone scrolling Twitter/X within the first second or two, makes them wonder what they are looking at, holds their attention to the end, and leaves them wanting to click through and play Yochi.

I want you to think very deeply about that objective before you begin making anything.

Do not simply assemble gameplay clips.

Do not create a traditional explainer.

Do not make a generic Web3 advertisement.

Do not make a generic esports intro.

Do not make something that resembles FIFA, Match of the Day, a TV sports ident, a crypto exchange commercial or an off-the-shelf cyberpunk motion graphics template.

Instead, build a coherent visual world around Yochi.

## The core idea

The strongest conceptual frame we have arrived at is that **markets become playable, social and competitive inside Yochi**.

A market candle is no longer something passively observed on a chart.

It becomes an event.

A moment is approaching.

Time is running out.

Players have to choose.

They commit to a side.

Other people are doing the same.

Someone will be right.

Someone will be wrong.

Money is at stake.

Winning happens publicly.

That tension is the emotional foundation of the trailer.

The candle should be an important recurring motif — almost the heartbeat of the world — but do not make the entire trailer revolve visually around one giant candle.

Use it intelligently.

It can represent uncertainty, time pressure, the moment of decision, transition and resolution.

It should help unify the film without becoming repetitive.

The deeper subject is:

**choice, pressure, rivalry, consequence, status and payoff.**

## What the viewer should feel

The viewer should move through something like:

**curiosity → tension → commitment → competition → escalation → chaos → payoff → desire to join**

Do not treat that as a rigid timeline or storyboard.

Decide for yourself how best to create that emotional journey.

I do not want to prescribe what happens at second 4, second 9 or second 18.

One of the reasons I am asking you to make this is because I want you to exercise your own visual reasoning, pacing, composition and creative judgement.

You have substantial creative freedom.

Use it.

## Yochi should feel social

One of the biggest hooks of Yochi is that this is not a player interacting with an anonymous machine.

People are competing against other people.

Wins have names attached to them.

Rivals emerge.

Streaks build.

Clans form.

Achievements accumulate.

Battle Royale players get eliminated.

A Duel is personal.

A large FFA feels collective.

Translate those ideas into visual storytelling rather than stopping the film to explain UI systems.

You might represent:

- players choosing opposing sides
- usernames appearing as identities
- rival figures facing one another
- clan symbols forming around groups
- streak energy building around a successful player
- players being eliminated from a Battle Royale
- status accumulating around repeat winners
- a crowd or surrounding world reacting to success
- one player emerging visibly above everyone else

These are examples, not requirements.

Invent stronger representations if you have them.

The important emotional idea is:

**winning happens in public.**

## Yochi should feel fun

This should not become grim, self-serious science fiction.

Yochi has personality.

It is internet-native.

It is slightly mischievous.

The film can exaggerate.

It can have visual humour.

It can use strange transitions, graphic character animation, playful destruction, dramatic reveals or moments that feel knowingly excessive.

I want it to be cool without becoming humourless.

Think:

**premium first, mischievous second.**

Roughly 80% style and conviction, 20% wink.

Do not make it a meme compilation.

But do not strip away the personality that makes Yochi different from a financial product.

## Winning USDC matters

Do not undersell this.

One of the strongest acquisition hooks is extremely simple:

**make a directional call, put USDC behind it, and win USDC if you are right.**

That should feel consequential.

Do not make a tacky get-rich-quick advertisement.

Do not use gambling-ad clichés.

But do make winning feel satisfying.

The audience should understand that there are real stakes.

A payout, balance increase, prize pool, animated USDC value, transfer of value or victorious reveal can all be useful.

Again, invent the best representation yourself.

The financial payoff should feel like part of the drama, not legal small print.

## Visual language

I want this to be **stylised animation**, not simulated live action.

Do not try to make photorealistic human characters.

Do not make uncanny AI people.

Do not imitate cinematic realism merely because it looks expensive.

This film is being made in code.

Treat that as an advantage.

You are free to use:

- 2D animation
- 2.5D
- procedural graphics
- vector illustration
- stylised characters
- cel-like animation
- graphic silhouettes
- abstract environments
- kinetic typography
- layered parallax
- procedural particles
- simulated depth and lighting
- shape morphing
- animated symbols
- dynamic camera work
- combinations of all of the above

Choose the visual language yourself.

The end result should feel coherent enough that every scene clearly belongs to the same film.

I am particularly interested in animation that feels **graphic and illustrative but still has depth, atmosphere and motion**.

Flat vector animation alone may feel too lightweight.

Fake photorealism would be wrong.

There is a large creative space between those two extremes.

Explore it.

The supplied avatar / achievement artwork can inform the tone if useful, but you are not obligated to reproduce it directly.

## Different forms of competition

Understand the emotional distinction between Yochi’s game modes.

FFA is many players collectively choosing sides around the same event.

Duels are direct, intimate confrontation between two players.

Battle Royale is escalation, survival, elimination and the final player remaining.

You do not need to label these modes or explain their mechanics in full.

Find ways to make their different emotional signatures readable through animation.

If you can imply all three in a way that rewards a second viewing, that is ideal.

## Motion

Motion should be one of the reasons this film is exciting.

I want the video to feel continuously alive without becoming visually exhausting.

Explore:

- snap zooms
- dramatic scale changes
- whip movement
- camera rotations
- perspective shifts
- parallax
- impact frames
- smear frames
- shape morphing
- hard graphic transitions
- motion trails
- rapid reframing
- freeze moments
- extremely fast sequences followed by sudden restraint
- elements crossing or breaking the frame
- typography interacting with the environment

Do not simply cut rapidly because “fast” equals “exciting.”

The motion should have purpose.

Rhythm and contrast matter.

A moment of near-stillness can be more powerful than another explosion.

## The music

Treat the supplied music as the **structural backbone** of the film.Do not think of the music as background accompaniment to animation. Think of the film and music as one authored object. The visual narrative should feel as though it could only have been made for this particular track.

I am providing a waveform and a timing map identifying the major musical moments.

Use those to understand where the strongest transitions, impacts, builds, pauses, drop and ending occur.

Do not mechanically cut on every beat.

That will make the film feel like a visualiser.

Instead, use the music to guide the overall flow and allow visual rhythm to breathe.

One specific creative opportunity I want you to consider:

**immediately before a major musical drop or resolution, reduce the visual movement dramatically.**

Create a moment of tension.

Perhaps almost everything freezes.

Perhaps the world waits for the candle to close.

Perhaps a competitor is suspended at the moment of commitment.

Then allow the next major musical impact to release that tension violently.

Do not use this exact implementation if you discover something stronger.

But understand the principle:

**contrast will make the payoff hit harder.**

The opening matters disproportionately.

Assume that the viewer owes us nothing.

The first frame should already feel intentional.

Study what makes outstanding short-form animation retain attention if useful.

Think critically about visual novelty, composition, rhythm and human attention.

## Duration

Use the supplied music track in full. It runs slightly over one minute, and its progression is important to the intended film. Build the visual narrative around the complete musical arc rather than shortening it simply to meet a conventional social-media runtime.
A one-minute film has to earn significantly more attention than a 20-second film. Treat retention as a creative problem. The opening still needs to command attention immediately, and the film should continue evolving visually and dramatically rather than settling into one aesthetic pattern. Every section of the music should create a new reason to keep watching.
If, after making and critically reviewing the complete film, you genuinely conclude that the full runtime weakens the work, you may propose a shorter alternate cut — but create the full version first.

This is not a strict requirement.

If the strongest version is slightly shorter or slightly longer, use your judgement.

Do not stretch an idea simply to reach a timestamp.

Do not compress something great simply because a number was specified.

The important thing is that every second earns its place.
